How to Resize Images to Any Dimension — The Right Way

Downscaling vs Upscaling — What the Software Actually Does

Resizing an image sounds simple, but the software faces a fundamentally different challenge depending on the direction.

Downscaling (making an image smaller) works well. The software averages groups of nearby pixels to create a smaller version. Detail is lost, but the result looks clean and natural. A 4000×3000 photo reduced to 800×600 will look sharp.

Upscaling (making an image larger) requires the software to invent pixels that do not exist. Traditional upscaling algorithms like bilinear and bicubic interpolation produce blurry, soft results. AI-based upscaling (Topaz Gigapixel, Adobe Enhance) performs dramatically better by predicting what detail should be there, but still has limits. As a practical rule: do not upscale more than 2× without specialised software.

Maintaining Aspect Ratio — and When to Break It

Aspect ratio is the proportional relationship between width and height. Common ratios include 16:9 (widescreen), 4:3 (older monitors), 3:2 (DSLR cameras), and 1:1 (square). If you resize to dimensions that do not match the original ratio, the image is either stretched or squashed. Always enable "lock aspect ratio" or "constrain proportions" unless you deliberately intend to change the shape.

To calculate a dimension manually: divide your target width by the original width to get the scale factor, then multiply the original height by that factor. Resizing 1920×1080 to 800px wide: 800 ÷ 1920 = 0.417. Height = 1080 × 0.417 = 450. Target: 800×450.

Understanding DPI and PPI for Print

Pixel dimensions determine on-screen quality. DPI (dots per inch) determines print quality. The standard for print is 300 DPI. At 300 DPI, the print dimensions in inches equal the pixel dimensions divided by 300. A 3000×2400 pixel image at 300 DPI prints at 10 inches × 8 inches. If you are only publishing to screens, DPI is irrelevant — what matters is pixel dimensions only.

Resampling Algorithms — Why They Matter

When software resizes an image, it uses a resampling algorithm to calculate the new pixel values. The choice of algorithm affects the sharpness and quality of the result, especially when downscaling significantly or upscaling at all.

Nearest neighbour is the fastest algorithm and the lowest quality. It simply copies the nearest existing pixel to fill each new pixel position. The result is blocky and pixelated — appropriate only for pixel art or situations where speed matters more than quality.

Bilinear interpolation averages the four nearest pixels to calculate each new pixel value. Results are smooth but slightly blurry. This is the default in most basic image tools and performs well for moderate resizing (up to 2× in either direction).

Bicubic interpolation considers the 16 nearest pixels, producing sharper results than bilinear with better edge definition. This is the standard in professional tools like Photoshop and is the right choice for most resizing tasks. PDFSnap uses bicubic resampling by default.

Lanczos is the highest quality algorithm, producing the sharpest results with minimal aliasing artefacts. It is computationally slower but the difference is visible on detailed images, fine textures, and text. Use Lanczos (available in GIMP and ImageMagick) for critical work where image sharpness is paramount.

What to Do After Resizing

Resizing alone does not always produce the sharpest result — it can introduce slight softness, particularly with downscaling. A mild sharpening pass after resizing recovers perceived sharpness. In Photoshop, use Filter → Sharpen → Unsharp Mask with Amount 60–80%, Radius 0.5–1px, Threshold 0. In any basic photo editor, a single application of "Sharpen" at low intensity achieves a similar effect. Apply sharpening as the last step before saving — never before resizing, and never on the master file.

After resizing, always compress the image before using it on a website. A correctly sized image at quality 80 JPEG will typically be under 200 KB, which is the practical target for web use. PDFSnap's workflow of resize then compress handles both steps without leaving your browser.
